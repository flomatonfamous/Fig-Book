## What Kind of Tree Is This?

You might be surprised to learn that the common fig, *Ficus carica*, actually belongs to
the mulberry family, which also includes breadfruit. Its native range stretches from
western Asia through the eastern Mediterranean, covering modern-day Turkey, Iran, and the
northern parts of Africa. People have been growing figs so long in so many places that
pinning down its exact origin is almost impossible. It adapted early and traveled far, and
the version we grow in American backyards today is essentially the same tree that fed
people in ancient countries thousands of years ago. The same fruit, biology, and the same
wonderful flavors, now spread across the entire world by emigrants and collectors.

In its natural form, the fig is a deciduous shrub or small tree, even one that can reach
twenty to thirty feet if you leave it alone. In the South, fig trees tend to grow in a
wide, multi-stemmed form rather than a single trunk, spreading outward as aggressively as
upward. Left unmanaged, they'll happily take over a corner of the yard, which can be seen
in old homesteads today. Most growers keep them pruned to something more manageable, but
the tree wants to grow big and wide. This is not a passive tree.

The leaves are unmistakable, even though patterns differ from variety to variety. Some
have large, deeply lobed leaves with three to five fingers, and some have large
spade-shaped leaves, but generally rough on top and slightly fuzzy underneath. They emerge
bright green in spring, deepen through summer, and drop in fall or sometimes in the heat
of summer. The shape of fig leaves is one of the most recognizable silhouettes in the
plant world. With the large leaves, it's probably the reason it was one of the only trees
to be mentioned by species in the book of Genesis and used as clothing.

Figs are reliably hardy from Zone 7 south, with some varieties surviving Zone 6 with
protection. They want heat, they want full sun, and they want well-drained soil. What
surprises most people is that despite loving summer heat, figs actually like to have a
dormant period in winter. They're not tropical plants, they're Mediterranean plants. That
distinction matters when you're figuring out how to grow them and how to protect them
through the winter. A fig that never goes dormant won't perform the way it's supposed to.
Container growers who keep trees in heated spaces year-round often wonder why production
disappoints them, and this is a big part of the answer.

**How fast do they grow?**

In the right conditions, figs are quick. A well-rooted cutting planted in the ground in
spring can put on four to six feet of new growth in its first full season in Zone 8 or
warmer. That same cutting in a container will grow more slowly because root development
and soil volume set the pace, but even in a five-gallon pot you can expect good growth
and often a small first harvest within the first or second year.

By the third year in the ground, most varieties in the South have established a root
system solid enough to handle drought, heat, and moderate winter with minimal
intervention. By year four or five, a well-managed in-ground fig tree in lower Alabama is
a productive, mature tree. Reaching real production in two to three years is one of the
reasons figs have always been a backyard staple in the South. You plant it one year and a
few years later you're getting plenty to eat from it.

**How long do they live?**

Under normal conditions, without periods of freezing temps into the teens or colder, a
fig tree will outlive the grower who planted it. Backyard fig trees in the American South
routinely reach fifty to a hundred years old. Old homesteads across Alabama, Georgia, and
Mississippi still have fig trees standing in yards where the house has long since fallen,
torn down, or abandoned. Those trees don't need anyone to water them or feed them or pay
attention to them, they keep growing and keep producing.

I know a tree about two miles from my orchard that's been doing exactly that for over a
decade, standing in a yard, surrounded by brush, and still fruiting. Some fig trees are
simply harder to kill than whatever was built around them. Understanding that kind of
longevity changes how you think about planting one. A fig tree is not a seasonal
experiment. It's something you put in the ground for the long run. So if you are
considering one for your yard, imagine what it will grow to become in 20 to 30 years.
After being established that long, you just don't dig it up and get rid of it. It develops
a root system you can't ignore.

**Root system**

Figs are surface feeders. Their root system is relatively shallow and spreading, extending
well beyond the drip line of the canopy. This matters for several practical reasons.

In the ground, shallow roots mean figs intertwine, reaching for water and nutrients in the
top layer of soil. They also make cultivation, hoeing, tilling, and digging near the base,
something to do carefully. Disturbing the surface root zone stresses the tree and can
trigger suckering at the base. A layer of mulch over the root zone is far better
management than bare cultivation.

In containers, the spreading, fibrous root system means a fig will fill a pot faster than
many other fruit trees. A tree that's running out of root room will tell you by slowing
growth, dropping figs before they ripen, and showing signs of stress even with regular
watering and feeding. Knowing this pattern means you'll recognize it when you see it and
respond with a pot upgrade or root pruning rather than trying to find a different
diagnosis.

For spacing in the ground, most varieties want at least ten to fifteen feet between trees
to give root systems room to develop without competing. Tighter spacing is workable but
will require more active management to keep trees productive.

## The Fruit That Isn't a Fruit

Here's the thing about figs that stops people cold when they first hear it: what you're
eating is not a fruit. It's something called a syconium, an inverted hollow receptacle
lined on the inside with hundreds of tiny flowers.

The flowers are inside the fig. You never see a fig tree burst into bloom the way an
apple or cherry tree does in spring, with clouds of petals and buzzing pollinators,
because the flowers are hidden inside what will eventually become the fig itself. The tree
is flowering, it just keeps everything internal.

When you slice a ripe fig down the middle, everything you see inside is the product of
that internal flower structure. The fleshy walls are the receptacle, ripened and swollen
with sugar and nectar that collectors refer to as fig honey. The pale or red lining inside
is the mass of tiny florets, each one fully developed. The small crunch in a ripe fig and
the satisfying texture that gives a good fig its depth comes from those tiny internal
structures, not from what we'd normally think of as seeds, though some may contain viable
seeds if pollination occurred.

This is also why fig varieties have such different interiors. The shape of that internal
chamber, the density of the florets, the color of the lining, all of it varies by variety.
Some figs open to a deep strawberry red. Some are pale gold. Some have an amber interior
that looks like liquid sunshine. That internal variation is part of what drives serious
collectors: every variety is not just a different-tasting fig, it's a different experience
right down to the structure of what's inside.

The ostiole is the small eye-like opening at the base of the fig, and it's the only gap
in the otherwise closed syconium. It plays a role in the fig's relationship with
pollinators and pests alike. On parthenocarpic varieties, which we'll get to in the next
section, the ostiole is usually tight enough to keep out most insects. On varieties with a
more open eye, it's a potential entry point for fruit flies, beetles, and rain. When
you're selecting varieties, ostiole tightness is something experienced growers pay
attention to, particularly in humid climates like the Gulf South.

## The Wasp Question

Ask almost anyone who grows figs long enough, and someone will bring up wasps. It happens
at plant sales, in online forums, and at family dinners after someone Googles where figs
come from. The question usually sounds something like: "Wait, aren't there wasps inside
figs?"

Here's the answer.

The fig wasp (*Blastophaga psenes*) does have a biological relationship with figs. Female
wasps enter caprifigs, the wild, non-edible male figs, to lay eggs, and in doing so they
carry pollen from one fig to another. This is a real relationship, one of the more
remarkable examples of mutual benefit in the plant world. But it applies specifically to
caprifigs and to a category of commercial varieties called Smyrna-type figs, which require
wasp pollination to develop fruit at all. The Smyrna fig that built California's early fig
industry required intentional caprification. Growers would hang branches of caprifigs in
the trees to ensure pollination. It worked, but it was a complicated system, and it's
essentially irrelevant to the home grower today.

The common fig varieties grown by backyard growers in the United States, including
virtually every variety in a typical collector's orchard, are parthenocarpic. That means
they develop fruit without any pollination. No wasp required. No wasp involved. The fruit
sets and ripens on its own. The biology driving your harvest has nothing to do with
insects, except that figs do attract the ones you don't really want, fruit flies.

I have a few caprifigs in my collection, the Stripped Butler, LSU DC-6, and Capri-Q, and
they're there for a specific reason. Caprifigs produce pollen, and for any grower
experimenting with varieties that benefit from it, having a pollen source on hand is
useful, and it changes the fig flavor but more on that later. Having a male fig is rarely necessary for the standard collection of common fig
varieties most growers collect.

For the record: in the rare cases where wasps do enter a fig, the fig's own enzymes break
down the wasp completely before the fruit is anywhere near ripe. There's nothing present
by harvest time. This fact doesn't make the conversation easier when someone brings it up
at Thanksgiving, but it's accurate.

The practical takeaway: grow common fig varieties, which is what virtually everyone does,
and the wasp question is interesting biology, but nothing you need to worry about.

## Breba Crop vs. Main Crop

A healthy fig tree can give you two harvests in one season. Knowing the difference between
them changes how you manage your trees.

The breba crop develops on wood from the previous season, the branches and stems that grew
last year and survived the winter. These figs start forming early and typically ripen in
late spring to early summer, often June in Zone 8. Breba figs tend to run bigger than main
crop fruit on many varieties, though the flavor can be a step behind depending on the
variety and the year. The breba is the appetizer. The main crop is the meal.

The main crop develops on the current season's new growth, the shoots that push out in
spring. These figs set later and ripen mid-summer through fall depending on variety and
climate. In Alabama, where summers are long and hot, main crop production can be really
good. Trees in large containers in Zone 8 can produce forty to eighty or more figs in a
season when conditions and nutrition are right.

Not every variety delivers both crops reliably. Some are known for heavy breba production,
while others barely produce breba at all and put their energy into the main crop. A few
produce so little breba that it's not really worth counting them as a two-crop tree. When
you're picking out varieties for a backyard orchard, it's worth knowing which type you're
working with, not because one is better than the other, but so you know what to expect
and when.

In colder climates where a fig gets cut to the ground by winter freezes, breba production
is gone, because there's no old wood left to carry it. That's one of the real reasons
winter protection of in-ground trees matters in borderline zones. Saving old wood isn't
just about saving the tree. It's about keeping one of its two harvests.

Knowing which wood produces which crop also shapes your pruning decisions. Heavy winter
pruning removes breba potential. Pruning that saves some old wood while encouraging new
growth gives you the best shot at both crops. We'll get into the specifics in the growing
chapter, but it's worth understanding the biology before you pick up a pruner.

## How Figs Ripen

Figs do not ripen off the tree. Most people figure this out the hard way, by picking one
too early and setting it on the counter waiting for something that never happens.

Once a fig leaves the branch, it stops sweetening. A fig that isn't ripe when you pick it
won't finish in a bowl on the kitchen table. It'll soften a little and then go bad, but it
won't develop the sweetness of a fig that finished on the tree. You have to let the tree
tell you when it's ready.

The most reliable sign is the neck droop. As a fig gets close to ripe, the weight of it
causes the stem to bend right where it meets the branch. A fig pointing outward or upward
still has time. A fig hanging straight down with a soft, drooping neck is right at or near
peak. Once you know to look for it, this one sign will save you more bad picks than
anything else you'll read in this book. You can also tell by the popular "FigCrack", when
the outside of the fig starts cracking and the skin splits a little, showing the white
flesh. Just don't let the moisture ruin it before you get to pick it.

Color is variety-specific but real. Brown and purple varieties get darker and the skin
softens. Green varieties pick up a yellowish tint or a slight golden look when they're
close. Yellow and gold varieties go more translucent. Most varieties also give a little
under gentle pressure when they're ready, not mushy, but not firm either. A rock-hard fig
has days to go. A fig that gives just a little under your thumb is close. One that
compresses with no resistance is either perfect today or a day past peak.

Some varieties put out a small bead of syrupy liquid at the ostiole, the little opening at
the bottom of the fig, when they're at peak. We call it fig honey. If you see it, pick
that fig now. Not tomorrow. Now.

Split figs are worth knowing about too. A small crack at the base of a very ripe fig,
especially after rain, is common. Split figs are usually at peak flavor but they have a
short window. Pick them and eat them that day. An open fig on the tree is an invitation
for beetles and mold, and they will take you up on it fast. If you've got rain coming
during harvest season, make it a habit to walk your trees every morning. You'll be glad
you did.

## The Milky Latex and What It Means

Break a fig branch. Scratch a leaf stem. Cut into green wood anywhere on the tree. A
white milky sap shows up almost right away. This is latex, a natural compound found
throughout the Ficus family, and it's the tree's way of protecting itself. Think of it as
the fig letting you know it didn't appreciate that.

For most growers, it's a minor thing to be aware of. The sap can irritate skin with
repeated contact, especially for people with sensitive skin. Most people won't notice it
much, but if you spend a long morning pruning or harvesting without gloves and keep
reaching your arms through the foliage over and over, you might end up with some redness
or itching on your forearms by the time you're done. A pair of thin gloves during a long
work session handles it.

For grafters, the latex is more of a practical issue. When grafting figs, the sap can get
between the scion and the rootstock and keep them from making a clean union. The fix is to
score the rootstock a little below your graft point and let it bleed out before you join
the two pieces. We'll cover grafting in the propagation chapter, but it's good to know
this now so it doesn't catch you off guard later.

The sap also behaves differently depending on the wood. Active new growth bleeds a good
bit when you cut it. Fully dormant wood in winter barely bleeds at all. Older established
wood falls somewhere in between. You can use this as a quick read on how active the tree
is when you're working on it, which is useful when you're doing timing-sensitive pruning
or taking propagation cuts.

One thing that surprises people: you won't taste or notice latex in a ripe fig. The sap
lives in the green wood and fresh cuts. By the time a fig is ripe and ready to eat, it's
not a factor.

## Why Figs Are Different From Every Other Fruit Tree

Put all of this together and you've got a tree that doesn't work quite like anything else
most people have grown.

There's no blossom season. You won't see a show of flowers in spring the way you do with
apple or peach trees. Fig trees just leaf out and start making fruit, no announcement, no
fanfare. One morning you walk out and there are small green figs showing up along the
branches. You didn't see them form. They're just there.

The two-crop setup means you're paying attention to different wood at different times of
year, old wood for the breba and new growth for the main crop. Pruning decisions and
winter protection decisions both affect what you harvest and when, in ways that don't
really apply to most other fruit trees.

The fact that figs won't finish ripening off the tree means your harvest lives outside, not
in your kitchen. You get in the habit of walking your trees in the morning during the
season, checking the neck droop, the color, watching for that bead of fig honey at the
tip. It becomes part of the daily routine. Honestly, it's one of the better parts of the
day.

The root system, the latex, the syconium structure, the dormancy requirement, the
two-crop biology, none of it is complicated once you know what you're looking at. But all
of it is specific to figs. General fruit tree knowledge gets you started, and then you
have to learn this particular tree on its own terms. That's a big part of what this book
is for.

Then there's propagation. A pencil-thick hardwood cutting stuck in good rooting medium
will become a productive fig tree. No special rootstock, no grafting required for most
varieties. Figs just root. That one fact is the reason the fig community runs on
generosity. When your tree gives you fifty cuttings every winter, you start handing them
out. The hobby runs on cuttings passed between people who've never met, mailed across the
country, traded at fig gatherings. No other fruit tree quite does it this way.

Figs tend to attract a certain kind of grower, someone who pays attention, who likes
learning the rhythms of a specific plant, who gets curious about the varieties and the
biology and the history. The tree is simple enough for a beginner and interesting enough
to hold someone's attention for thirty years. That's a hard combination to find, and it's
a big part of why the fig community keeps growing.
