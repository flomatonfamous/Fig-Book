## Opening — Randall's Voice (polished from author's original text)

When I think about where my fig journey truly began, my mind doesn't go to orchard rows
or propagation benches or even my nursery today. It goes back to a single Brown Turkey
fig tree planted in my grandmother Mildred's backyard.

That tree was part fruit source, part climbing structure, part childhood playground — and
entirely hers. My grandfather Claude didn't pay much attention to it, but she treasured
it like a member of the family. Every Sunday, when my cousins and I piled out of the car
and tore across the yard, she'd already be one step ahead of us, gathering what ripe figs
she could before we got our sticky little hands on them.

Those early memories — warm summer air, the soft pop of a fig skin splitting between my
fingers, the sweetness of a fruit that somehow tasted like sunshine and childhood combined
— anchored something deep in me long before I realized it. My introduction to figs wasn't
through horticulture or research or rare varieties. It was through love, family, and the
kind of simple Southern living that teaches you more than you ever appreciate at the time.

That old tree still stands today, even though the homestead is abandoned, overgrown, and
long since sold to someone who never moved in. Vines crawl up the trunk now, and brush is
trying its best to swallow the yard whole, but that fig tree refuses to give up its place.
I visit it a few times a year — not out of habit, but out of a sense of connection. I pick
fruit. I gather a few cuttings. I breathe in the quiet and remember where this whole
passion began. Every time I'm there, I feel like I'm standing between generations — my
grandmother behind me, my own orchard in front of me.

It's impossible to look at that tree without feeling its history. And the more I've learned
about figs over the years, the more I've come to understand that this feeling isn't unique
to me. Figs have been intertwined with human life — food, faith, survival, and culture —
since the earliest pages of recorded history. My story with figs may start in a backyard
in Alabama, but the larger story began thousands of years before that. And understanding
that story changes the way you grow the tree.

So before we get into varieties, pruning, fertilizer, or orchard design, let's take a step
back and look at where this remarkable fruit comes from. To grow figs is to participate in
something ancient.
