# Chapter 1: The Tree That Started Everything

**STATUS: APPROVED — May 24, 2026 (revised by Randall June 4, 2026; Mechelle + community update June 5, 2026)**
**Word count target: 3,500 | Actual: ~3,150**

---

When I think about where my fig journey truly began, my mind doesn't go to orchard rows
or propagation benches or even my nursery today. It goes back to a single Brown Turkey
fig tree planted in my grandmother Mildred's backyard.

That tree was part fruit source, part climbing structure, part childhood playground, and
part family heirloom. My grandfather Claude didn't pay much attention to it and could probably
care less about it along with a few members of the family, but she treasured
it. Every Sunday around the first part of July when the figs were in season, I couldn't wait to
make my way around behind the house to that fig tree. My cousings and I would climb out on limbs
and several feet up into the air to get the largest and ripest fruit.

Those early memories of warm summer air, the soft pop of a fig skin splitting between my
fingers, the sweetness of a fruit that somehow tasted like sunshine and childhood combined,
anchored something deep in me long before I realized it. My introduction to figs wasn't
through horticulture or research or rare varieties. It was through love, family, and the
kind of simple Southern living that teaches you more than you ever appreciate at the time.

That old tree still stands today, even though the homestead is sitting vacant, the tree overgrown, and
long since sold to someone who never moved in. Vines crawl up the trunk now, and brush is
trying its best to swallow the yard whole, but that fig tree refuses to give up its place.
I visit it a few times a year not out of habit, but out of a sense of connection. I pick
fruit. I gather a few cuttings. I breathe in the smell and remember where this whole
passion began. Every time I'm there, I feel like I'm standing between generations.

It's impossible to look at that tree without feeling its history. And the more I've learned
about figs over the years, the more I've come to understand that this feeling isn't unique
to me. Figs have been intertwined with human life — food, faith, survival, and culture 
since the earliest pages of recorded history. My story with figs may start in a backyard
in the panhandle of Florida, but the larger story began thousands of years before that. And understanding
that story changes the way we grow the tree.

So before we get into varieties, pruning, fertilizer, or orchard design, let's take a step
back and look at where this remarkable fruit comes from. To grow figs is to participate in
something ancient.

---

## A Fruit That Outlasted Empires

Archaeologists digging near the Jordan Valley have uncovered evidence of fig cultivation
stretching back roughly eleven thousand years, making the fig one of the oldest cultivated
food plants ever documented. What makes that number remarkable isn't just its size. It's
what it means: people were tending fig trees before they were planting wheat fields. Before
organized grain farming, before the earliest cities, there were fig trees being deliberately
grown and cared for. Something about this fruit compelled people to settle near it,
cultivate it, and make it part of their lives from the very beginning.

From those early cultivations in the ancient mediteranian, the fig tree made its way into the
daily life, religion, and literature of every major civilization that followed. We know the Egyptians
grew figs because they appear in tomb paintings alongside grain and honey as essential
provisions for the afterlife. In ancient Greece, figs were a staple food woven into culture
and mythology. Greek athletes consumed them as a training food, and the fig held a place in
religious traditions. When Rome rose to dominate the ancient world, figs came with it. 
The Romans had a sacred fig tree — the Ficus
Ruminalis — that stood at the heart of their founding mythology, and the fruit itself was a
common and valued part of Roman life from the tables of senators to the rations of soldiers.

But no ancient civilization gave the fig more a prominent a place than the one documented in
the Bible. The fig tree is one of the only trees specifically named in the Garden of Eden.
When Adam and Eve reached for something to cover themselves after the fall, they grabbed
fig leaves. That single detail makes the fig tree the first plant
named by its species in human history's most widely read account of the world's beginning.

The fig shows up all through Scripture beyond Genisis. Across the Old
Testament, sitting "under one's own vine and fig tree" was a consistent picture of peace and God's blessing,
an idea for what a good life was supposed to look like.
That phrase appears in Kings, in Micah, in Zechariah, more than once, which tells you how
thoroughly the fig was woven into everyday life in that part of the world.
Figs were grown beside peoples homes, fed their families, and shaded their afternoons. In the New Testament,
Jesus used the fig tree in parables and teachings such as the barren fig tree.

Whether your interest in figs is historical, botanical, or faith. This is a tree that has mattered to people for as long as people
have exsisted.

## From Ancient Groves to American Backyards

The fig didn't stay in the Middle East. Like everything valuable, it traveled, carried by
traders, settlers, missionaries, and ordinary people who wanted to bring along the plants
that fed them and mattered to them.

It spread west through the Mediterranean over thousands of years, moving with Phoenician
traders, Greek colonists, and Roman expansion until it had taken root across North Africa
and southern Europe. By the time exploration opened the New World, the fig was as
embedded in Mediterranean culture as the olive. Spanish missionaries carried fig cuttings
to California in the eighteenth century, planting them at the missions they established
along the coast. Europeans brought cuttings with them from places like Italy, France, and Spain.
The variety they brought became so tied to that history that it still
carries the name today. The Mission fig, dark-skinned, dense, and sweet, is still one of
the most widely recognized fig varieties in the country.

Figs made their way to the American South through a similar process, brought by settlers
and homesteaders who packed cuttings alongside seeds and tools, carrying the plants that
had fed their families wherever they came from. The South turned out to be good fig
country. The summers are long and hot, which is exactly what figs want, and varieties like
Brown Turkey and Celeste proved hardy enough to handle Southern winters without much help.
They established easily, grew large, and produced reliably year after year with almost no
attention at all.

Almost every old homestead had a fig tree somewhere in the yard. They weren't ornamental and they
weren't a hobby — people used them for preserves and enjoyed them on hot biscuits on a cold winter morning.
Figs were put up as preserves every summer, jarred
in the heat of summer as the garden vegetables were finishing up and being stored. Nobody thought much about variety
names or collection strategies. You had the tree you had, usually grown from a cutting
passed along by a neighbor or a relative, and it fed your family. That's almost certainly
how my grandparents brown turkey arrived, from someone nearby who had more than they needed and passed a rooted cutting along.
That's how fig trees have always moved between people.

That informal tradition of sharing is still alive today, though the fig world around it
has changed a lot. Serious collectors now build collections of hundreds of varieties. There
was a time when most of that community lived on a forum called Figs for Fun, where
collectors swapped cuttings freely just because they loved the fruit. That forum is retired
now, but the spirit carried forward. Today there's Ourfigs.com, dozens of Facebook groups
dedicated entirely to fig growing and trading, and a whole culture of collectors comparing
notes online the same way people used to share cuttings across the fence. Auction platforms
like FigBid have created an active market for rare and sought-after varieties, and university
research programs have developed entirely new lines of figs bred specifically for American
growing conditions.

Louisiana State University's AgCenter ran one of the most significant of those programs,
producing a series of varieties that have become staples for Southern growers. LSU Purple,
LSU Gold, LSU Champagne, LSU Scott's Black, and several others came out of that work,
each one bred for performance in the heat and humidity of the Gulf Coast. LSU Purple in
particular has become one of the most widely planted backyard figs in the region, valued
for its reliable production and cold tolerance. Among fig collectors, the LSU series has
real credibility. They were bred for this climate, and they deliver. Several of them are
in my orchard, some I like better than other, some withstand the humid south better than other.

The fig's journey from ancient Jordan Valley cultivation to a breeding program in Baton
Rouge to a backyard tree in your neighborhood is a long and a continuous one.
The same impulse that led ancient societies to tend figs, that led missionaries to pack
cuttings for a new continent, that led homesteaders to share rooted starts with
their neighbors, and now suburban collector growing them on there patio and storing them in 
their garage, that same impulse drives the fig community today. People find this fruit,
fall for it, and want more of it. Then they want to propagate it and share it. That part hasn't changed in
thousands of years.

## What This Book Is

This is a practical book about growing figs, but it's also more than that. It's about
what figs mean when you actually grow them: the patience of waiting for a cutting to root,
the satisfaction of biting into that first fig that you've heard so much rave about, the particular pleasure of growing something with
real history and maybe a cool story behind it.

Whether you're starting with a single container plant on a
patio or building a collection of a hundred varieties, the fundamentals here will serve
you. We'll cover varieties and how to choose them, how to plant, feed, and prune, how to
propagate more trees than you'll know what to do with, how to get through winter, and how
to harvest figs at their absolute best. There's a chapter on building a serious collection
and one on turning a backyard passion into a small nursery business, because for some of
us, that's exactly where this goes.

The goal is a book you'll read and enjoy, and maybe learn from, one that you will often reference
rather than sitting on a shelf, written by someone who grows and enjoys the tree, the fruit and sharing stories with strangers and hasn't forgotten
what it was like to start down this rabbit hole.

None of this happens alone. My wife Mechelle is right there with me through it all,
from laying out the orchard, which she takes full credit for, to picking out varieties that we should add, the tastings and even the traveling to events. She even helps with boxing up trees and sending them off in the mail to customers. She has strong opinions about figs. Her favorite is Saint Rita, and Domnick's Italian and she has
never been shy about telling anyone either. She's my partner in all of this.

If you want to follow along beyond these pages, I document the orchard, the nursery, and
the adventures and misadventures on our YouTube channel at Flomaton Famous. If you haven't found us yet, I invite 
you to check us out and join along on our many adventures beyond just figs.

As they often say, Let's jump right in.
