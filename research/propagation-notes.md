## Overview

Figs are among the easiest fruit trees to propagate vegetatively — which is one of the
reasons the fig community is so generous. When something roots this readily, sharing
becomes second nature. The four main methods are: hardwood cuttings, air layering, grafting,
and seed. For practical growers and nursery operators, the first two cover 95% of real-world
use. Grafting has its place. Seeds are mostly for breeders.

---

## Method 1: Hardwood Cuttings

### Why this is the standard method
Cuttings are fast, low-cost, and scale easily. One good tree can produce dozens of
propagatable cuttings each season. Done right, rooting success rates in the 70–80% range
are realistic, and experienced growers regularly do better than that.

### Timing
The best window is late winter through early spring, while the tree is still dormant or
just waking up. In Alabama, that typically means late January through March. Cuttings
taken after leaf-out work too, but you lose more of them — the tree is spending energy on
top growth instead of root initiation.

Many growers cut in late fall or winter, wrap cuttings in a damp paper towel, seal them
in a plastic bag, and refrigerate them until planting time. This is a common and reliable
storage method that lets you bank cuttings from winter pruning and plant them on a schedule
that works for you.

### What to cut
Pencil-thick hardwood from the previous season's growth. Straight, healthy wood with no
sign of disease or frost damage. Cut pieces 6–8 inches long. Most growers cut straight
across the top and at an angle at the bottom — this helps with orientation later (you
always know which end goes down) and makes it harder to accidentally plant it upside down.

### Rooting medium
Drainage is the key. Waterlogged cuttings rot — they don't root. A mix of 50% perlite
and 50% potting soil, or coco coir with perlite, works well. Pure perlite or a
perlite/sand combination is also effective. The medium should hold moisture but drain
freely. Small individual pots or deep cells (4x9 tree pots work well for nursery use)
are standard.

### Rooting hormone
Optional, but it doesn't hurt. Figs root on their own, but a dip in rooting hormone —
either powder or liquid IBA — can improve consistency and speed things up, especially
with harder-to-root cultivars. Keep the powder thin — applying too much can actually
encourage rot rather than rooting.

### Environment
Warm and humid. Bottom heat in the 77–80°F range is ideal. Ambient warmth works too,
especially indoors or in a heated greenhouse. Keep humidity high around the cuttings
while roots are establishing — a humidity dome, a clear plastic bag over the pot, or a
misting setup all work. Once you start seeing new leaf growth pushing from the top,
roots are usually developing below. At that point, start weaning them off high humidity
gradually.

Indirect light during the rooting phase is fine. Direct sun can stress an unrooted
cutting before it has the root system to compensate.

### What to watch for
New leaf growth is a promising sign — but it doesn't guarantee rooting. Fig cuttings
can push leaves on stored energy before roots develop. The real test is a gentle tug: if
the cutting resists, roots are there. If it pulls free easily, it still needs time.

## Method 2: Air Layering

### Why it works so well
Air layering doesn't cut the branch from the tree until after roots have already formed.
That means the developing plant is still being fed by the mother tree the whole time —
water, nutrients, sugars. The stress on the new plant is minimal. Success rates approaching
90–100% are routinely reported, and the resulting plant is already a good size with a
real root system before it ever leaves the tree.

### Timing
Spring through midsummer, while the tree is actively growing. Roots form fastest during
warm, long days. In Alabama, May through July is prime territory. Starting a layer in
late summer risks running out of warm weather before the roots mature — you need roughly
6–10 weeks of warmth after wrapping.

### How to do it
1. Select a healthy branch about pencil to finger thickness. Somewhere in the middle of a
   long, strong branch works well.
2. Remove bark in a ring about an inch or so wide — a complete girdle of bark and the
   green layer beneath. This interrupts the flow of sugars past that point, which signals
   the tree to produce roots at the wound.
3. Optional: dust the exposed wood with rooting hormone powder before covering.
4. Wrap the wound with a generous handful of moist sphagnum moss — the moss should be
   damp, not dripping.
5. Wrap the moss tightly with clear plastic film. Seal top and bottom with tape or grafting
   strips. The goal is to keep the moss moist and dark.
6. Check every week or two. If the moss is drying, inject a little water or unwrap and
   re-moisten.
7. When a healthy root mass is visible through the plastic — typically 4–8 weeks in warm
   weather — cut the branch off below the roots, pot it up, and keep it shaded and humid
   for a week or two while it adjusts.

### Aftercare
Newly detached air layers are in a little bit of shock. A shaded spot, consistent moisture,
and a few weeks of patience get them through it. Don't push them into direct summer sun
immediately. Once they push new growth confidently, they're established.

### Advantages for the nursery
Air layers produce mature-looking, well-rooted plants fast. They tend to establish more
quickly than a same-aged cutting-grown tree and can fruit sooner. For rare or hard-to-root
varieties, this method can be a lifesaver when cuttings aren't cooperating.

## Method 3: Grafting

### When grafting makes sense
Figs root so easily that grafting isn't necessary for propagation. Where grafting becomes
useful is variety change — adding a desired variety to an existing tree, or converting an
unproductive fig into something better. Topworking an old tree by grafting multiple
varieties onto it is one application. Saving scion wood from a tree that's been damaged
or is about to be lost is another.

### Timing
Late spring is the window. The rootstock needs to be leafing out and actively growing,
and the weather needs to be warm — consistently above 65–70°F is important. Fig grafts
won't callus properly in cold conditions. Growers who keep dormant scion wood refrigerated
through winter and graft in mid-spring get reliable results.

### Technique notes
Figs bleed milky latex sap when cut, which can interfere with cambial contact at the graft
union. A useful trick: make shallow scoring cuts below the graft site a day or two ahead
of time, or immediately before grafting, to let the sap bleed out and reduce pressure at
the union. Clean, sharp tools and quick execution help too.

Common methods include cleft grafts, whip-and-tongue grafts, and bark grafts for larger
stock. The scion and rootstock cambiums must align — at least on one side if not both.

After joining, wrap the graft union tightly with grafting tape or parafilm. Seal the scion
to prevent it from drying out before the union takes. Some growers wrap the entire scion
in parafilm for this reason.

### Success rates
When conditions and technique are right, fig grafts take well — 80–90% success in
experienced hands. Cold conditions, poor cambial alignment, or latex interference are the
main reasons for failure.

## Method 4: Seed Propagation

### Honest assessment: not for most growers
Figs grown from seed won't be true to type. Common fig varieties are clones, and seeds —
when viable — produce genetically variable offspring with unpredictable fruit. Seedlings
take two to three years or more to fruit, and the result is a mystery until they do.

This is a breeding tool, not a production tool. Plant breeders use seed propagation to
create new varieties. Backyard growers and nursery operators don't need it.

If you do want to try it — for curiosity or breeding — fresh seeds from a fully ripe,
pollinated fig germinate best when kept consistently moist and warm (around 75–80°F).
Spring sowing is the right time. The seeds are tiny and surface-sown on a fine, sterile
germination mix.

|--------|----------------|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Hardwood cuttings | 70–85% | Late winter – early spring | Low | Standard propagation |
| Air layering | 90–100% | Spring – midsummer | Medium | Rare varieties, large plants |
| Grafting | 80–90% | Late spring | High | Variety change, topworking |
| Seed | Variable | Spring | Medium | Breeding only |

## Notes for Nursery Operations

- Cuttings in 4x9 tree pots are the standard nursery production unit — inexpensive,
  rootable in quantity, and shippable once established.
- Air layers work well for producing specimen-size plants of high-value varieties.
- Tracking rooting dates, variety, and batch size from the start makes inventory management
  much easier — especially when scaling up.
- Winter cutting collection during annual pruning is an efficient workflow: prune, cut,
  bag, refrigerate, and plant in spring when greenhouse or outdoor conditions are right.

## Fig Propagation and the Community

The fig collecting community runs largely on cuttings. Growers swap, sell, and trade
cuttings constantly — on Facebook groups, FigBid, and at local swaps. The ease of fig
propagation is a direct cause of the hobby's generosity: when a tree produces 50 rooting
candidates every winter, sharing is easy. This culture of exchange is part of what makes
the fig community different from most other fruit tree hobbies.
