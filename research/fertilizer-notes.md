## The Big Picture: Figs Are Not Heavy Feeders

One of the most common mistakes backyard fig growers make is over-fertilizing. Figs are
not gross feeders. A mature, healthy fig tree that's putting on 12–18 inches of new shoot
growth per year is telling you it has what it needs. If you're seeing that kind of growth
and getting fruit, your fertilizer job is already done. Adding more won't make the figs
sweeter — it'll just make the tree bushier and less productive.

The primary nutrient figs actually need supplemented in most soils is nitrogen. Phosphorus
and potassium are usually present in adequate amounts in average garden soil. This is why
balanced fertilizers — not high-phosphorus bloom boosters — are the standard recommendation
for figs.

## Soil Basics Before Fertilizer

Figs do best in well-drained loamy soil with healthy organic matter content. They have
relatively shallow root systems, which means what's happening in the top foot or two of
soil matters a lot. pH sweet spot is roughly 6.0–6.5, though figs tolerate a fairly wide
range (approximately 5.5 to 8.0). Outside that range, nutrient availability starts to
suffer even when fertilizer is present — so a soil test before starting a fertilizer
program is always worth the small investment.

Heavy mulching is one of the best things you can do for a fig. A 4–6 inch layer of
organic mulch around the tree conserves moisture, slowly feeds the soil as it breaks down,
and protects shallow roots. Many extension recommendations actually say: at planting time,
skip the fertilizer and focus on compost and mulch. Let the tree settle before you start
feeding it.

## NPK Ratios: What to Use

### Balanced is usually best
For in-ground figs, balanced formulas work well: 8-8-8, 10-10-10, and 13-13-13 are all
commonly recommended by extension services across the South. These give the tree all three
macronutrients without overshooting nitrogen.

### Higher nitrogen for growth
When a fig has been cut back hard — from a freeze, heavy pruning, or starting from a small
tree — a higher-nitrogen formula (something in the range of 20-10-10 or similar) can
jumpstart regrowth. Use it temporarily to get the tree moving, then back off to a balanced
ratio once growth is established.

### Lower nitrogen when fruit is the goal
If a tree is growing aggressively but producing few figs, pull back on nitrogen and let the
tree shift its energy toward fruiting. Some growers switch to a lower-nitrogen or
fruit-set formula in midsummer for exactly this reason.

### What you already have in the garage
Most gardeners have 13-13-13 or 10-10-10 on hand already. Either works fine for figs.
If you have a high-nitrogen lawn fertilizer, use it carefully and sparingly — it'll push
growth but can work against fruiting if overused.

## Organic vs. Synthetic

Both work. Many experienced growers use a combination.

**Organic options (compost, manure, cottonseed meal, fish emulsion, blood meal):**
- Improve soil structure and microbial life over time
- Release nutrients slowly and steadily — lower risk of burning
- Naturally supply micronutrients the tree needs
- Compost and aged manure respond particularly well on figs

**Synthetic options (granular balanced fertilizers, slow-release pellets, liquid feeds):**
- Immediately available to the plant
- Easier to control precisely
- More important for container figs, where potting mix has lower microbial activity
- Risk of root burn if over-applied

One practical approach: top-dress with compost annually to build soil health, and
supplement with a measured dose of granular synthetic fertilizer during key growth windows.

## Container Fig Fertilizing

Potted figs need more frequent feeding than in-ground trees because:
1. Their root zone is limited
2. Frequent watering leaches nutrients out of the pot quickly

**General approach:**
- Feed every 4–6 weeks through the active growing season (roughly spring through midsummer)
- A balanced liquid fertilizer at half-strength works well for containers
- Slow-release granular fertilizers (incorporated at spring potting time) can simplify
  the routine
- Stop feeding by late summer — potted figs in cold climates need to harden off before
  being moved inside for winter

Watch for signs: yellowing or unusually small leaves usually mean the tree is hungry.
Vigorous dark-green growth with no fruit often means too much nitrogen.

Salt buildup is a container issue — flush the pot thoroughly every few weeks by watering
heavily until water runs freely from the drain holes. This keeps salt concentrations from
building to levels that harm roots.

## In-Ground Fig Fertilizing: Timing by Season

### Late winter / early spring
The most important feeding window. Apply before or just as buds begin to swell. This
feeds spring leaf-out and fruit bud initiation. For young trees, this might be the only
application needed. For established trees in poor soil, it's the biggest application of
the year.

### Spring through early summer
Young trees or trees recovering from dieback may benefit from a second or third split
application — one in late spring and one in early summer (no later than mid-July in most
Southern climates). Split feedings reduce leaching in rainy conditions, which is a real
factor in Alabama's summer weather.

### After midsummer — stop
No fertilizer from late summer through winter. Late-season nitrogen pushes soft new growth
that won't harden before cold arrives. It can also interfere with fruit ripening. The
tree needs to slow down, ripen its crop, and prepare for dormancy.

## How Much to Apply (General Guidelines)

Scale to tree size. A newly planted fig needs very little — focus on soil prep, not
fertilizer. As the tree grows:

- **Year 1:** Light feeding only, if at all. A small amount of balanced fertilizer in
  late spring once the tree is actively growing.
- **Years 2–5:** A few ounces of balanced fertilizer split over 2–3 applications per
  season.
- **Mature tree (6+ years):** One good feeding in late winter/early spring is often
  enough. Scale up based on tree size, but excess fertilizer on a mature fig does more
  harm than good.

Spread fertilizer under the canopy, starting about a foot away from the trunk and working
outward to the drip line. Scratch it in lightly or water it in well — shallow fig roots
appreciate it being accessible near the surface.

## What the Tree Tells You

This is the most important diagnostic tool you have:

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| 12–18 inches of new growth, good fruit set | Fertility is right — don't change anything |
| Less than 12 inches of growth, pale leaves | Tree may be underfed — increase nitrogen |
| Vigorous growth, lots of leaves, few fruits | Likely over-fertilized — dial back nitrogen |
| Fruit won't ripen, stays green | Could be excess nitrogen or drought — reduce feeding and check water |
| Small or pale new leaves | Possible nutrient deficiency — feed lightly and observe |

## Micronutrients

Healthy soil and a good organic matter program cover most micronutrient needs automatically.
In alkaline soils (more of a western U.S. issue), iron and zinc deficiencies can appear —
yellowing young leaves with green veins is a classic sign. Chelated iron applied to soil
or as a foliar spray addresses this. Alabama's naturally acidic soils rarely cause this
problem, but it's worth knowing if you're recommending practices to growers in other regions.

## Regional Notes for the Book

- **Deep South (Alabama, Gulf Coast):** Heavy summer rainfall leaches nutrients quickly.
  Split applications work better than one big dose. Watch for fig rust in late summer —
  fertilizing too late makes this worse by keeping growth soft into fall.

- **Northern growers:** Shorter season means lighter, earlier feeding. Stop by June in
  many cases. Container overwintering makes late feeding especially dangerous.

- **California / arid west:** Dry climate means less leaching. A single late-winter feeding
  often carries the tree through the season. Manual irrigation must be provided after
  fertilizing since rain won't do the work.
