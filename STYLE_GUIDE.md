# Fig Book — Style Guide

> These are the canonical writing rules for every chapter of this book.
> The ghostwriter must follow these before drafting any section.
> **Also read:** `research/randall-voice-profile.md` (Randall's voice from manuscript + YouTube transcripts).

---

## Voice & Tone (Randall's voice — locked May 26, 2026)

- Write as if Randall White is speaking directly to the reader — warm, direct, experienced
- Avoid generic AI gardening language ("ensure," "vibrant," "thriving," "bountiful," "lush")
- Do not open paragraphs with "I" (vary sentence structure)
- The book should feel like it comes from someone who has actually grown figs in Alabama heat,
  propagated hundreds of cuttings, and spent real time around trees
- Practical comes first. Inspiration follows from practical truth — not from abstract cheerleading
- **Em-dashes: sparingly, maximum 2–3 per chapter.** Default to commas. Only use an em-dash in one of three situations: (1) a pivot where a comma creates a run-on or splice, (2) a mid-sentence list that needs visual separation from surrounding text, (3) a reveal or punchline at the end of a clause where a comma buries it. Never use an em-dash just for stylistic variety or to make prose feel more dramatic. Emotional and personal passages stay comma-driven.
- **No superlative adverbs** (dramatically, remarkably, entirely, etc.).
- **7th–8th grade reading level.** Common talk, real examples, light humor — not dramatic.
- See `research/randall-voice-profile.md` for canonical sentence examples from Randall's own edits.

## Randall's Structure (from Chapter 1 revision — June 2026)

- **Personal → historical → practical → invite.** Memory and family first; history as context; then what the book does; soft close.
- **List rhythm:** "part X, part Y, part Z" for characterizing trees, places, or the hobby.
- **Short beats in emotional passages:** "I pick fruit. I gather cuttings. I breathe in the smell."
- **Bring history home:** after civilization/biblical sweep, land on Southern homestead reality (preserves, biscuits, passed-along cuttings).
- **Honest hedging on varieties:** not all LSU (or any series) varieties are equal — "some I like better than other."
- **Colloquial is fine:** rabbit hole, heard so much rave about, care less, misadventures.
- **Closings:** folksy and forward — "Let's jump right in" — not formal summary.

## What the Tone Is NOT

- Not preachy or over-inspirational
- Not dry or overly academic
- Not a Wikipedia article with a warm sentence tacked on
- Not generic "here's how to grow figs" content that could have been copied from anywhere

## Sentence Style

- Prefer shorter, cleaner sentences over complex run-ons
- Use plain language. If a simpler word exists, use it.
- Use real specifics: variety names, container sizes, temperatures, timelines — not vague generalities
- Occasional longer sentences are fine for storytelling passages — but keep them under control

## Biblical & Historical Sections

- Treat with accuracy and respect
- Do not invent historical or biblical facts
- Flag anything that needs external verification before it goes in the final draft
- Biblical references should feel natural and connected to the larger story — not pasted in like a devotional
- The Garden of Eden fig connection is a key thematic thread through the book

## Personal Stories

- Only include stories Randall has confirmed or provided
- Do not invent family details, experiences, or events
- When a personal story slot exists in an outline, note it as [RANDALL'S STORY: topic] and ask before drafting

## Paragraph Length

- Avoid walls of text
- 3–5 sentences per paragraph is a good target for most sections
- Longer paragraphs are acceptable for narrative or storytelling passages

## Headers & Structure

- Use clear section headers within chapters
- Readers should be able to skim headers and get a sense of what the chapter covers
- Do not over-subdivide — keep the book feeling like a readable narrative, not a manual

## Chapter Length

- Chapter 1 length is locked as-is. Do not shorten.
- Later chapters may exceed Chapter 1's word count to make up volume.
- Target: chapters feel complete and satisfying, not artificially padded or cut short.

## Vocabulary Watch List

Avoid these overused AI phrases:
- "vibrant," "lush," "bountiful," "thriving," "robust" (in the generic gardening sense)
- "delve," "explore," "journey" (as a metaphor for reading the book)
- "ensure," "utilize," "leverage" (bureaucratic language)
- "as we know," "it's worth noting," "it goes without saying"
- Starting a chapter with "In this chapter, we will..."

## Post-Draft Voice Check
*(Run this on every section before moving to the next one)*

### Step 1 — Banned Phrase Scan
Search the draft for these and rewrite every hit:
- Hedge qualifiers: "it's important to note," "many growers find," "in general," "generally speaking," "it should be noted"
- Politeness inflation: "wonderful," "exciting," "a great option," "transformative," "nuanced"
- Generic gardening voice: "vibrant," "lush," "bountiful," "thriving," "robust," "flourish"
- Bureaucratic filler: "ensure," "utilize," "leverage," "facilitate," "implement"
- AI padding: "delve into," "let's explore," "on this journey," "as we've discussed"
- Adverb problems: dramatically, remarkably, entirely, incredibly, simply (when used as emphasis, not meaning)

### Step 2 — AI Structure Check
Read the section at the paragraph level. Ask: does every paragraph have an intro sentence, three balanced points, and a wrap-up? That pattern signals AI-generated structure. Fix it deliberately:
- Break one paragraph into a single sentence standing alone
- Let one paragraph run longer than the others
- Cut the wrap-up sentence from at least one paragraph and let the last point be the last point

### Step 3 — Specificity Check
Flag any sentence that could have been written about any plant by any author. The test: if you replaced "fig" with "tomato" and it still reads fine, it's too generic. Every section should contain at least one:
- Specific variety name
- Specific place, date, or measurement
- Something only Randall would know from his own orchard or experience

### Step 4 — Rhythm Check
If every paragraph in the section is 3–5 sentences, break that pattern on purpose before finishing. Add a one-liner. Let something run long. Read the section aloud and listen for monotony.

### Step 5 — Read-Aloud Gate
Required before marking any section complete. Read it out loud as if talking to another grower standing in the orchard. Flag every sentence that:
- You wouldn't actually say out loud
- Sounds like a brochure or press release
- Uses vocabulary you'd only see in a written gardening article, not a conversation

Rewrite those sentences until they pass. This step catches what every other check misses.
